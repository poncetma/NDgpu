§ 1

The physics problem

A reactor is critical when neutron production by fission exactly balances losses to absorption and leakage. NDgpu solves the standard multigroup neutron diffusion model of that balance: neutron energies are collapsed into G groups, and within each group g the scalar flux φg(r) obeys

−∇·(Dg∇φg) + Σr,g φg  =  Σg′≠g Σs,g′→g φg′  +  (χg/k) Σg′ νΣf,g′ φg′

with Dg the diffusion coefficient, Σr,g the removal cross section (absorption plus out-scatter), Σs,g′→g the scattering matrix, νΣf fission production and χ the fission emission spectrum. Written abstractly this is a generalized eigenvalue problem, Mφ = (1/k)Fφ: the loss operator on the left, the fission source on the right, and the largest eigenvalue k = keff is the criticality measure — k = 1 is a self-sustaining core.

Two refinements share all the machinery below. The SP3 solver replaces each group's single diffusion equation with two coupled diffusion-type equations in the angular moments (φ0+2φ2, φ2), capturing the leading transport effects that plain diffusion misses (steep gradients, strong absorbers, small cores) at 2–3× the cost. The transient solver adds the time derivative (1/vg)∂φg/∂t and delayed-neutron precursor families, for power excursions and control transients (§ 4.3).

§ 2

Discretization: the stencil, explained

2.1Finite volumes and the two-point flux

The domain is divided into cells, and the diffusion equation is integrated over each cell. The divergence term turns into a sum of neutron currents through the cell's faces, so the balance for cell i reads: removal inside the cell plus net outflow through its faces equals the source. The only approximation needed is the current through one face. With cell-averaged fluxes φi, φj in the two cells sharing a face of area A, whose centroids are a distance d apart, the two-point flux approximation is

Ji→j ≈ D̃ · (φi − φj) / d · A,   D̃ = 2DiDj / (Di + Dj)

The harmonic mean D̃ is not a stylistic choice: it is the unique face coefficient for which the flux is continuous across a material interface, exact for piecewise-constant D. It also does the physically right thing at a strong absorber (D → 0 chokes the face; an arithmetic mean would leak through). It is implemented once, as harmonic_mean() in ndgpu/operator.py, and used by every lattice.

Dividing by the cell volume gives one coupling coefficient w = D̃A/(dV) per face. The per-cell balance is then a weighted difference of the cell against its immediate neighbours — a stencil:

(Aφ)i = (Σr,i + Σf∈faces wf) φi − Σf∈faces wf φnbr(f)

On a Cartesian grid every cell has 6 neighbours (a 7-point stencil); on the triangular lattice 3 in-plane neighbours (+2 axial in 3D); on the hexagonal lattice 6 in-plane (+2 axial). The geometry enters only through A, d, V:

For the triangle, for instance: face length h, centroid spacing h/√3, area (√3/4)h², so w = D̃·h / ((h/√3)·(√3/4)h²) = 4D̃/h². The discretization is second order: halving the cell size cuts the k error by four (verified in § 6).

2.2"Shifted multiply-adds": the stencil as array algebra

The stencil defines a huge sparse matrix — one row per cell, seven entries per row. NDgpu never builds it. The observation is that on a structured lattice, the west neighbour of every cell is the same array shifted by one index. So applying the whole operator to a flux array phi is a handful of elementwise operations on shifted array views. This is the Cartesian operator's entire apply():

out = diag * phi                     # removal + sum of face couplings
out[1:, :, :]  -= wx * phi[:-1, :, :]   # west  neighbour, all cells at once
out[:-1, :, :] -= wx * phi[1:, :, :]    # east
out[:, 1:, :]  -= wy * phi[:, :-1, :]   # south
out[:, :-1, :] -= wy * phi[:, 1:, :]    # north
out[:, :, 1:]  -= wz * phi[:, :, :-1]   # below
out[:, :, :-1] -= wz * phi[:, :, 1:]    # above

Each line multiplies two contiguous arrays and subtracts into a third — one fused "multiply-add" kernel over millions of cells. That is what the code and README mean by the stencil applied as shifted multiply-adds. The coupling arrays wx, wy, wz and the diagonal are precomputed once per group; material heterogeneity, active-core masks and boundary terms are all baked into them, so the hot loop contains no branches, no index lists, and no matrix. The triangular and hexagonal operators are the same idea with different shift patterns (the down/up triangle sublattices couple at three fixed offsets; hex cells at six axial-coordinate offsets), and the 3D extrusion adds exactly the two wz lines.

Non-lattice-filling cores (a hexagonal reactor on a rectangular array) use an active mask: face couplings into excluded cells are zeroed, so inactive cells decouple entirely, and each exposed face picks up the boundary term below. The excluded cells simply ride along as inert unknowns — cheaper on a GPU than compressing them away.

§ 3

Boundary conditions: one Robin law

Every boundary in NDgpu is the single Robin law Jnet = α·φs — the net outward current proportional to the surface flux — with the physics selected by α:

ConditionαMeaning
reflective0zero net current: a symmetry plane, or an idealized perfect reflector
vacuum (Marshak)½no neutrons return from outside; the standard diffusion vacuum condition
albedo β(1−β)/2(1+β)a fraction β of outgoing neutrons comes back (homogenized reflector)
zero-flux∞φ = 0 on the surface (Dirichlet limit)

The face term follows from the same two-point idea as § 2.1: couple the cell centre to the (unknown) surface flux with the current J = (2D/d)(φc − φs), set it equal to αφs, and eliminate φs. What remains is a pure diagonal contribution

leakage / volume = 2Dα / (d(dα + 2D))

implemented once as robin_face_term() and specialized only in its geometric prefactor for hex and triangular faces. α → ∞ recovers 2D/d², the zero-flux term; α = 0 contributes nothing. Because the boundary only ever adds a non-negative number to the diagonal, none of this disturbs the operator properties that the solvers rely on next.

§ 4

The solvers

4.1Outer loop: power iteration on the fission source

The eigenvalue problem is solved by the classic power iteration. Given a flux guess, compute the fission source Fφ = Σ νΣf,gφg; solve the within-group equations with that source frozen; the ratio of new to old total source updates k. Groups are swept Gauss–Seidel style — downscatter uses the freshest fluxes — and iteration stops when both the change in k and the L2 change of the normalized source shape are below tolerance. Power iteration converges at the dominance ratio of the reactor (slowly for large, loosely coupled cores) but is robust, matrix-free, and every iterate is physical.

4.2Inner loop: conjugate gradients, and why it is allowed

Each group sweep needs one linear solve with the within-group operator Ag. By construction Ag is symmetric positive definite: symmetric because face couplings are shared pairwise (cell i sees −w·φj exactly as j sees −w·φi); positive definite because each diagonal equals the sum of the couplings in its row plus removal and boundary terms — diagonal dominance with strict surplus wherever neutrons are absorbed or leak. That licenses conjugate gradients, the optimal Krylov method for SPD systems, whose iterations are exactly the operations of § 2.2 plus dot products — no factorization, nothing stored. Jacobi (inverse-diagonal) preconditioning costs one more elementwise multiply and handles the removal-scale contrast between materials.

Two economies matter more than any single iteration: the inner CG tolerance tracks the outer residual (early outers are solved crudely, final ones tightly), and every solve is warm-started from the previous outer iterate — so late outer iterations cost only a handful of stencil applications. The SP3 solver reuses everything: multiplying its second moment equation by 5 makes the per-group 2×2 block system symmetric, and one CG runs on the stacked (Φ1, φ2) state.

4.3Transients: backward Euler with analytic precursors

The time-dependent solver marches with backward Euler — first order but unconditionally stable, the right default for stiff reactor kinetics. Each precursor family's ODE is solved analytically within the step and substituted back, so each time step is a single fixed-source multigroup problem whose operator is the steady one plus a positive diagonal shift 1/(vgΔt) — still SPD and better conditioned, so the same CG machinery applies. The initial steady state is "critically adjusted" by 1/k0 so an unperturbed core stays exactly flat. Near criticality the within-step fission-source fixed point contracts arbitrarily slowly, so it is Anderson-accelerated: each sweep's update is replaced by the residual-minimizing combination of the last few iterates, collapsing the slow modes in a few sweeps.

§ 5

Why this is fast on a GPU

The stencil apply reads a few arrays and writes one, doing ~2 floating point operations per value read: its arithmetic intensity is far below what any modern chip can compute, so the solve is memory-bandwidth bound. That dictates the whole design:

  • Matrix-free beats sparse. A stored sparse matrix (CSR) spends most of its bandwidth on column indices and irregular gathers. The shifted multiply-adds of § 2.2 read only flux and coefficient values, in perfectly contiguous order — the access pattern GPUs coalesce best.
  • Device-resident state. Fluxes, sources and coefficients live on the GPU for the entire solve; the only host↔device traffic is one scalar convergence check per CG iteration.
  • One code path. Everything is written against the NumPy API surface that CuPy mirrors, so device="cpu"|"gpu" selects the backend and the physics code is byte-for-byte identical. The CPU test suite therefore exercises the same code that runs on the GPU; float32 roughly doubles GPU throughput at ~10−6 accuracy in k.

All numbers in this report were produced on CPU. The GPU path is exercised on Colab (notebooks/colab_gpu_benchmark.ipynb); by design it produces the same answers, faster.

§ 8

The HP-MR microreactor model

The target application: the ANL/INL HP-MR heat-pipe microreactor reference design (2 MWth, NEAMS Virtual Test Bed). The geometry was decoded from the published Serpent model: 30 hexagonal TRISO fuel assemblies around a central shutdown-rod cell, one ring of beryllium reflector hexes alternating with 12 rotating control drums — beryllium cylinders carrying a boron-carbide absorber arc. Rotating the arcs toward the core is the reactor's control mechanism.

§ 9

Limitations & roadmap

  • Cross sections are placeholders. The HP-MR numbers exercise geometry and solver; predictive worths need the SPH-corrected two-group constants being prepared in FEMFFUSION — they drop in through ndgpu.read_xsec / read_material_xml and the materials= hook, with conventions verified against FEMFFUSION's own parsers.
  • Diffusion near strong absorbers. A 1 cm B4C arc is exactly where diffusion theory is weakest; SPH correction (or the SP3 option, not yet wired to the triangular lattice) is the standard remedy.
  • Arc rasterization. The absorber annulus is thinner than a triangle below refine 4 (the builder refuses); drum-worth studies want refine ≥ 6. Worth converged to ~1% between refine 6 and 8.
  • Air treated as vacuum outside the reflector ring, and the drums' hex-corner slivers as drum body — both standard, both small.
  • Next: drum-rotation transients (the machinery already accepts the triangular operators), tri-lattice SP3, GPU timing of the 3D core on Colab, Wielandt-shifted outer iterations for large cores.
